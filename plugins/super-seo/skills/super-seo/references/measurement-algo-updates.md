# Measurement, Testing, and Algorithm Updates

Everything else in an SEO program is a hypothesis until measurement says otherwise, and most SEO measurement is broken in ways invisible from inside the tool. Google Search Console is the only first-party dataset a site owner gets, and its default views are engineered to be un-actionable. Rank trackers sample somebody else's keywords in somebody else's city. Split testing is the only method that produces causal evidence, and it needs traffic most SaaS sites do not have. Meanwhile Google ships core updates roughly quarterly and spam updates two or three times a year. This note covers what can be measured, what can be tested, what the 2024 API leak changed in practice, and how to triage a drop without burning six months on the wrong diagnosis.

## TL;DR

- GSC's UI caps at 1,000 rows; the API returns up to 25,000 per call with `startRow` pagination, supports an `hour` dimension and `dataState: "hourly_all"`, and admits it returns "top ones" not all rows; the BigQuery bulk export has no cap because nothing is requested, Google just writes every row daily (developers.google.com, support.google.com).
- The API-to-BigQuery gap is not marginal: 40,000 unique queries via API versus 350,000 via BigQuery on one property, and on a new page 24 clicks / 252 impressions / 47 queries via API versus 79 clicks / 44,274 impressions / 5,384 queries in BigQuery (advancedwebranking.com). API keyword discovery runs on a roughly 9x truncated sample.
- Macro CTR and macro average position are worthless: sitewide average position 19.6 can decompose to 1.0 on one query and 38.5 on another. The metric only means something filtered to one query AND one page (YT The Edward Sturm Show, David Quaid) [consensus]. Cannibalization has a matching signature: filter to one query, drop the page filter, find gaps in the daily impression series where one URL falls out and another appears, then re-run over 7 days to strip Google's rotation tests.
- Split tests are the only causal instrument and the entry bar is hard: roughly 30,000 organic sessions/month to the tested page group, hundreds of same-template pages, server-side implementation, 2 to 4 weeks to significance (searchpilot.com). Homepages, one-off pages and sitewide changes are untestable.
- Title tests keep winning and so does deletion: brand to front +15%, location to front +8.5% at 95% confidence off a ~0.7% CTR lift, all-caps +14% mobile and flat on desktop, product carousel removed +29%, embedded map removed -7% (searchpilot.com).
- Volatility indexes sample a fixed, US-weighted, daily, blue-links-only keyword set: Mozcast 10,000 keywords across 5 US cities, Semrush Sensor a fixed set across 25+ categories in 9 countries. The June 8-12 2026 divergence, practitioners screaming while every dashboard stayed flat, is the canonical failure (digitalapplied.com).
- HCU recovery numbers should anchor expectations: of 390+ sites Glenn Gabe tracked from the September 2023 HCU, 81 (21%) showed any recovery by late August 2024, and most sat 10-20% above prior-year rather than back at baseline (seranking.com citing gsqi.com).
- The leak's durable change is not "clicks matter". It is index stratification with a sliding link-equity scale per tier, making the only honest proxy for a link's value whether the linking page itself ranks and gets traffic, not its DR/DA (YT The Edward Sturm Show, Mike King).
- Manual action versus algorithmic demotion is a 30-second binary: a Search Console notice means reconsideration; a clean report means nothing to appeal and recovery waits for the next update pass (ppc.land).

### Google Search Console: the instrument, and how to stop misreading it

David Quaid's demolition on The Edward Sturm Show starts from an r/SEO post reading "Total clicks 443, impressions 74,000, CTR 5.7%, average position 6.4. Are these good?" The question is unanswerable. Search is inbound and context-driven, not a push feed like YouTube or TikTok where view rate decides a video's fate, so a property-level CTR averages across queries with unrelated intent and unrelated positions. On his own property, aggregate average position 19.6 decomposed to 1.0 for his name, 4.5 for a near-variant and 38.5 for "top SEO experts". GSC disables the CTR and position checkboxes by default on some views for exactly this reason.

**The click zone.** CTR optimization only exists inside what Quaid calls the click zone: roughly positions 1 to 3 for small specific terms, 1 to 7 for big terms, maybe out to 16 on the largest. Outside it, "your page title could be blank for all that matters". That kills the most common reporting fallacy, attributing a CTR change to a title rewrite on a page that never entered the zone.

**Impressions are not demand.** He walks a case where a page published in February took its first impression for a target query in June, because the query only started being searched then, after he talked about it on podcasts. Two or three impressions a day on a hyper-specific term means a handful of humans typed it after hearing it. Conversely a page losing impressions is often one Google briefly promoted, tested and demoted, so impressions fell because position fell out of a visible slot, not because demand moved.

**Agent autonomy:** fully autonomous. Filtered query+page decomposition, click-zone classification, and "did impressions move because position moved or because demand moved" are deterministic transforms over GSC rows. The highest-value automation here, because the manual version is tedious and therefore skipped.

### Getting past GSC's ceilings

**The three tiers.** The UI caps at 1,000 rows on every tab. The Search Analytics API accepts `rowLimit` 1 to 25,000 (default 1,000) with zero-based `startRow`, dimensions of country, device, page, query, searchAppearance, date and hour, `type` of web / image / video / news / googleNews / discover, and `dataState` of `final`, `all` or `hourly_all`, with the load-bearing caveat that it "does not guarantee to return all data rows but rather top ones" (developers.google.com). The BigQuery bulk export is a push, not a pull, so the daily row limit does not apply (support.google.com).

**What the gap costs.** Advanced Web Ranking's January 2024 comparison: API reported 893,691 clicks with 48,613 branded and 387,984 non-branded, leaving roughly half unaccounted for; BigQuery reported 923,847 total (3.4% higher), 50,189 branded, 523,744 non-branded and 349,914 clicks explicitly labelled anonymized, at a query cost of 0.0016 EUR [single-source, mechanism documented].

**Anonymization does not go away.** Google withholds queries issued by very small numbers of users over a two-to-three month window. BigQuery does not un-anonymize them, it exposes them as rows with `is_anonymized_query = true` and a null query, so you can quantify the hole instead of silently losing it. For SaaS the consequence is specific: API-based long-tail discovery is blind to exactly the emerging, low-volume, high-intent queries you most want.

**Hourly data.** The 24-hour view shipped December 2024 with hourly clicks, impressions, CTR and position across Search, Discover and News; the export followed in early January 2025, with incomplete points marked by a dotted line (X @googlesearchc, searchenginejournal.com). Gabe's framing is the useful one: hourly GSC lets you watch an update land the way his hourly GA4 method did (X @glenngabe). Related trap: when Google fixed an impressions bug, Gabe noted the fix lowers future impressions while historical data stays uncorrected, so baselines break silently (X @glenngabe).

**Agent autonomy:** BigQuery setup is a one-time human gate (GCP project, billing). After that every pull and sweep is an agent job. The API path needs no gate but must be labelled a sample, not a census.

### Regex and segmentation recipes

GSC uses RE2: partial matching unless anchored with `^` or `$`, case-insensitive by default with `(?-i)` forcing sensitivity, no lookaheads, lookbehinds or backreferences, patterns capped around 4,096 characters. Filters live under Performance > Search results > New filter > Query or Page > Custom (regex), with both "Matches regex" and "Doesn't match regex" (support.google.com, seocrawl.ai). Recipes worth a recurring job: brand versus non-brand as one pattern including misspellings plus its negation, the single most important segmentation and the one nobody maintains; question intent via `^(who|what|when|where|why|how|does|can|is|are|did|should|could)\b`, where AI Overview cannibalization shows first; section comparison via `/blog/|/docs/|/compare/` on Page, diffed against each other rather than the site total; the comparison surface via `vs |versus |alternative|competitor`, usually SaaS's highest-converting and most under-built cluster; and residue, meaning "Doesn't match regex" against brand plus your top 50 head terms, leaving the genuine long tail. Quaid writes regex by hand while noting the GSC AI assist button now produces filters faster than typing them, which is moot for an agent.

**Agent autonomy:** fully autonomous. Store patterns per site in Postgres, version them, re-run on schedule so the brand pattern grows as misspellings surface.

### Rank tracking and volatility indexes

Semrush Sensor scores 0 to 10 by category and device, above 7 meaning real industry-wide movement, and hit 9.5 during the February/March 2026 back-to-back window. Mozcast uses a weather metaphor over a fixed set. Both refresh daily.

**The structural blind spot.** Digital Applied's post-mortem on June 8-12 2026 is the clearest statement of the failure mode: practitioners reported the loudest turbulence in months while every aggregate dashboard stayed calm. Not a malfunction, a sampling artifact with four causes: geographic blindness (US-weighted samples miss an EU-only movement), vertical aggregation (a single-industry crash averages away across 25+ categories), daily cadence (one reading smooths intraday movement), and blue-links-only measurement (AI Overview citation churn invisible). Their figures: Mozcast 10,000 fixed keywords across 5 US cities measuring only blue links; Sensor a fixed set across 25+ categories in 9 country databases, also ignoring AIOs; Grump, Algoroo, Sistrix and AWR structurally similar. The window followed the May core update completing June 2 after an 11-day rollout, with no Google confirmation.

**The two-layer rule.** External indexes tell you whether the SERPs are broadly volatile; your own tracking tells you whether you are affected. Neither answers alone. Barry Schwartz's running posts are context, not diagnosis, which his own phrasing concedes: "volatility continues to be heated without a confirmed core update" (X @rustybrick).

**Localization.** Rollouts are not simultaneous or equal across device and country: during the May 2026 core update the German desktop reading stayed Low throughout while German mobile repeatedly spiked High. A tracker on one location and one device measures a projection of the change, not the change [consensus].

**Agent autonomy:** an agent can maintain its own index over its own tracked keywords, which beats any public index because the sample is yours. Per country, per device, per page-type cluster, daily, storing the distribution rather than the mean. The mean is what makes public indexes blind.

### SEO split testing: the only causal instrument

Split one template's pages into statistically similar control and variant buckets, apply the change server-side to the variant only, forecast what the variant would have done absent the change from the control's live performance plus their pre-test historical relationship, then test significance. SearchPilot replaced its earlier approach in 2019 with a purpose-built neural network, Split Optimizer, trained on the site's own seasonality to detect smaller uplifts at a given confidence level.

**Entry requirements are the whole story.** Roughly 30,000 organic sessions per month to the tested group, hundreds of pages on one template, server-side or edge implementation so Google sees the change immediately. Two to four weeks to significance, trends often visible inside a week. Untestable: single pages, homepages, sitewide changes that hit both buckets. Their cloaking answer is that there is only one version of each page and both users and Googlebot get it (searchpilot.com).

**Tests that won**, with the caveat that a vendor publishing its customers' results is a biased sample: brand to front of titles +15% (retail); dynamic pricing in titles +10% against a static-pricing variant at -7%, which is the more interesting half; location to the very start of the title +8.5% at 95% confidence off a ~0.7% CTR lift (healthcare); all-caps titles +14% mobile with no significant desktop change (travel); "Updated Daily" in titles +11% (listings); unique human content replacing templated content +14%; product descriptions moved above the fold out of tabs +14%; product carousel removed from category pages +29%; H2s changed to question format +12%; AI-generated content added to informational text +13% US and inconclusive in Australia; pros/cons table added to comparison content +50%; embedded video review carousel removed +4.1% on brand PLPs; embedded map removed from local listing pages -7%.

**What the pattern says.** Title structure is the highest-yield testable lever and roughly half the wins come from removing something. The market split on the AI-content test and the device split on all-caps titles are why borrowed best practices are worthless: the same change on the same site produced different outcomes across markets and devices [contested as a general rule, single-vendor evidence].

**Agent autonomy:** not end to end. An agent can bucket, forecast and test significance and propose tests; deployment is a human gate and the traffic floor rules out most early-stage SaaS. Below the floor the honest substitute is a before/after against a matched control set of untouched pages, reported as directional.

### Correlation studies and where they stop

Zyppy's 50-site case study, from a 4,000+ site database with Ahrefs metrics, shows the genre and its limits. Correlations for sites losing traffic: anchor text variety per page at -0.337 internal and -0.352 external; date freshness at 0.455 for winning, where winners averaged 774 days since last update against 273 for losers and 35% of winning URLs carried no date against 4% of losers; numbers in titles -0.297; adjectives in titles -0.420. No meaningful correlation: Google traffic reliance (-0.143), author information (-0.041), table of contents (0.042), word count (-0.161), Google Business Profile (-0.156). Cyrus Shepard's own framing is that correlation is not causation and the sample is very small (zyppy.com). The nulls on author info and word count are the ones worth carrying, being negative findings against two of the loudest prescriptions in the field [contested, small sample]. Mike King's complaint is adjacent: after the leak and the DOJ testimony "the SEO software industrial complex has not changed in any meaningful way", and what the field should run is vertical-specific correlation studies on clickstream and feature extraction rather than recycled DR/DA (YT The Edward Sturm Show).

### Forecasting founders trust, and measuring three things not one

Every credible 2026 method converges on a range with named scenarios, not a point. Flying Cat's model uses 3 to 4 months of recent traffic as baseline rather than a full year (historical patterns being less predictive now), rankings and volumes, CTR curves segmented by position and query type, conversion rate by segment, and content capacity. Ramp assumptions: new content 3 to 6 months to full potential, technical fixes 1 to 2 months, major site changes an expected dip first. Three scenarios, a 10 to 15% volatility buffer, and a ±15% variance threshold triggering a re-forecast (flyingcatmarketing.com). The load-bearing line is "use real CTR data from your own GSC rather than industry benchmarks": their 2026 figures put position 1 at 57.33% average CTR against the widely-cited 20-30%, AIOs present in 10.58% of studied keywords, position 1 at 46.88% with AIOs versus 35.17% without, and positions 9-10 at 281% higher CTR with AIOs present [single-source, and the position-1 number runs opposite to the prevailing narrative].

Mike King's answer to "what do we even measure now" is the framework that survives the shift to AI surfaces: stratify into performance measurement (referral traffic and what it does on-site), brand or channel visibility measurement (visibility, citation rate, citation accuracy, which he calls the big one), and input metrics (what you can actually move: bot activity, rankings for synthetic queries, passage relevance scores, entity salience, other NLP metrics). Most teams measure only the middle bucket, call it visibility, and stop. His firm uses Profound plus internal tooling for the gaps (YT The Edward Sturm Show). The input bucket is the one an agent owns most naturally, because its numbers come from instrumentation you control rather than a platform's report.

**Agent autonomy:** autonomous with a human gate on assumptions. The agent maintains the baseline, derives the site's own CTR curve from GSC rows, re-runs scenarios monthly and flags ±15% breaches. It should not pick which scenario becomes "the number".

### The algorithm update record, 2023 to 2026

Roughly 22+ confirmed updates from January 2023 through April 2026, about one core update per quarter plus two or three spam updates a year (digitalapplied.com). The dated record, with third-party timelines disagreeing on some end dates: 2023 core March 15-28, reviews April 12-25, core August 22-September 7, **Helpful Content Update September 14-28**, spam and core overlapping October 4-20, core November 2-28. 2024 **core plus spam March 5-April 19**, the update that folded the helpful content system into core ranking and introduced the site reputation abuse policy, then spam June 20-27, core August 15-September 3, core November 11-December 5, core December 16-24. 2025 core March 10-27, spam April 7-22, core June (Gabe dates it June 30, completing in 16 days), spam August 18-September 1 (first confirmed spam update since December 2024), core September 8-25, reviews November 3-18, core December 11-29 over 18 days. 2026 a Discover-specific core update from February 5, a first; core in March; spam March 24-April 5; core May 21-June 2 over 11 days, which Gabe summed up as "March was meh, but May is big" (X @glenngabe); spam in June; spam August 18-21 completing in slightly over two days.

Two rules fall out. **Movement before the announced start is not the update:** Gabe checked with Google directly after widespread claims about March 6 movement, and the answer was that the update launched when announced, so anything earlier was something else (X @glenngabe). **Adjacent updates counterbalance**, so the net hides the story: Gabe documented sites surging on the November or December core updates and reversing hard on the December spam update (X @glenngabe), meaning a single before/after window spanning two updates reads as "nothing happened" when two large opposite things happened. His method is the one to copy: a fixed panel of previously-impacted sites, at one point over 3,000, measured identically every time.

### Helpful Content aftermath and what recovery looked like

Of 390+ sites Gabe tracked from the September 2023 HCU, 81 (21%) showed recovery as of August 28 2024, nearly a year later. James Brockbank's panel of 671 travel publishers found 46 (7%) hitting 50% traffic increases in the first week of the August 2024 core update. Lily Ray observed one site at about 80% recovered; the majority of "recovered" sites had regained only 10-20% of prior-year traffic (seranking.com, aggregating gsqi.com and others). Marie Haynes' March 2024 read was blunter: she had yet to see a meaningful recovery from a clear September HCU drop (X @Marie_Haynes).

The June 2025 core update is the clearest documented recovery event. Gabe reports HCU(X) sites surging on July 8-9, well into a rollout that began June 30, meaning Google updated specific systems partway through rather than at launch. Not universal: some dropped further, and most recoveries were partial (gsqi.com). The mechanism as Google describes it is a site-wide classifier: the proportion of unhelpful content must fall heavily and stay fallen for months, then a core update pass picks it up. No notification, no reconsideration, no confirmation when it lifts [consensus]. Gabe's remediation framing is "kitchen sink" after a delta report establishing whether the drop is a relevancy shift or a quality problem. The delta step is what people skip and it decides everything: a relevancy shift, where Google now wants a different page type, needs a different page, not better content on the existing one.

### Spam updates and site reputation abuse

Gabe's four case studies from the August 2026 spam update (August 18-21) are content-based, not link-based: a programmatic-plus-AI ultra-YMYL site that lost rankings for 200,000+ queries, which he calls "a two-headed monster"; a fully programmatic Amazon thin-affiliate site that lost 14,000 queries entirely, content detected at 100% AI-generated probability; a site with 1.5M+ indexed URLs of which roughly 85% were programmatic, hit site-wide, with aggressive ads; and a 250,000-URL scaled-content site in a high-risk niche with malicious redirects that lost roughly 25,000 queries. His warning: "I would be very careful when scaling AI-generated content or employing programmatic content" (gsqi.com).

Site reputation abuse was announced March 5 2024, manual enforcement began May 7 2024, and enforcement moved toward algorithmic through the August 2025 spam update. On August 30 2026 the policy split: outside the EEA a manual action "will directly affect search results for the portion of the site affected", while inside the EEA Google separates the section so it "ranks independently from the rest of the site" with no ranking penalty, prior EEA manual actions were removed, and pages are not disadvantaged by having had one. Google published four evaluation factors: presentation consistency with the host, content quality relative to the main domain, stated or implied authorship and editorial responsibility, and whether identical content appears elsewhere (searchenginejournal.com). For SaaS the read is narrow but real: guest-post programs, sponsored directory sections and "partner content" subfolders are judged on those four, and editorial responsibility is the one most companies cannot honestly claim.

### The 2024 API leak: what actually changed in practice

Mike King published his teardown on May 28 2024 (X @iPullRank), and the discourse has since collapsed into "clicks matter, site authority exists". The findings that changed practice are narrower.

**Index stratification and the sliding link-equity scale.** The index splits into tiers (high, medium, low, plus fresh docs) and link value scales with the tier the linking document sits in. King's framing: the field believed whether a linking site gets traffic or ranks does not matter for link building, and "we found that it's definitively true that that matters". The proxy for tier is whether the linking page itself ranks and gets traffic. On vendor metrics he is unambiguous: "all these like domain authorities, domain ratings, and so on, like they don't even matter. They've always been these kind of like entertainment metrics" (YT The Edward Sturm Show). Directly actionable and directly measurable: score link prospects by whether the specific linking URL ranks for anything. His second shift was how much Google uses vector embeddings at page, site, entity and person level, rolled up across levels, implying the link graph matters less than assumed while nobody outside Google holds an equivalent index to reason with.

**Attributes worth knowing** (ipullrank.com): `siteAuthority` feeding the Q* ranking system; NavBoost with `goodClicks`, `badClicks`, `lastLongestClicks`, `unsquashedClicks`; `chromeInTotal` for site-level Chrome views; `hostAge` explicitly sandboxing fresh spam at serving time, with `createdDate` and `expiredDate` on a January 1 1995 epoch; `titlematchScore`, with no character-count measure anywhere except a snippet prefix; `OriginalContentScore` as a 7-bit 0-127 value applied only to pages with little content; `siteFocusScore` and `siteRadius` for topical cohesion and drift; `homePageInfo` trust levels NOT_TRUSTED / PARTIALLY_TRUSTED / FULLY_TRUSTED; `sourceType` tying anchor value to index tier; three date signals (`bylineDate`, `syntacticDate`, `semanticDate`); 2,596 modules and 14,014 attributes total.

**Twiddlers.** King's explanation is the cleanest available: re-ranking functions applied after initial scoring, each a boost or a demotion, making "plus 50, minus 50" adjustments on a feature, with Panda one of them before being folded into core, which is what "we brought it into the core algorithm" actually means (YT The Edward Sturm Show). For triage this matters: a Twiddler-shaped demotion is a step function on one document class, so it shows in GSC as a cliff confined to one page type, not a gradual slide. Charles Floate's circulated timeline of contradictions (2016 "no website authority score" versus `siteAuthority`, 2019 "no click data" versus NavBoost on Chrome clickstream) is accurate and mostly rhetorical (X @Charles_SEO). The leak produced no new tactics; it produced a defensible basis for rejecting vendor metrics and treating click behaviour as a real objective [consensus among practitioners, contested by Google].

### Penalty versus devaluation triage

Run in order. Under an hour, and it prevents the most expensive mistake in SEO, remediating a penalty that does not exist.

1. **Check the Manual Actions report.** A notice means a human applied an action, named by violation type, usually with sample URLs, scoped partial (URLs, directories, subdomains) or site-wide. A clean report means no penalty and no reconsideration path. More than a dozen categories exist, including unnatural inbound and outbound links, thin content, cloaking, sneaky redirects, keyword stuffing, third-party and user-generated spam, 16 News and Discover subcategories, plus site reputation abuse (2024), back button hijacking (2026) and undisclosed incentivized reviews (2026). Structured-data violations leave content indexed but disable the markup, so rich results vanish with no ranking change. Reconsideration takes "several days or weeks", longer for link cases, and pages must be crawlable with no login wall, robots.txt block or noindex (ppc.land).
2. **Align the drop date against the confirmed update calendar.** Inside a rollout window it is algorithmic; outside every window it is more likely technical, an indexing event, a SERP-feature change or a demand shift.
3. **Decompose demand from ranking.** Impressions flat and clicks down means CTR moved, usually a SERP feature appearing above you. Impressions down and position flat means the query stopped being searched. Impressions down and position down means you lost ranking. Mechanical, and the thing a GSC-connected agent should compute on every alert.
4. **Check concentration.** Loss confined to one template or directory points at a Twiddler-style demotion or a section-level action; loss spread evenly points at a site-wide classifier or a site-level authority change.
5. **Check whether you lost rankings or lost the query.** Pull current SERPs for the top lost queries and classify the page types now in the top five. A relevancy shift presents identically to a quality demotion in aggregate charts and needs a completely different response.

Recovery paths diverge completely. Manual action: fix, file reconsideration, action lifts on approval. Algorithmic: nothing to file, re-evaluation happens on Google's schedule, in practice the next core update pass after months of sustained change (ppc.land, gsqi.com).

### Kill, rehab, or migrate a domain

Thin, mostly anecdotal evidence, so treat this as a decision framework. **For rehab:** backlinks are the slowest asset to rebuild, and a high-authority link pointing at a URL that 404s after a relaunch loses both the referral traffic and the equity it passed internally; full URL mapping with 301s preserves that [consensus]. **For a fresh domain:** some HCU-hit owners abandoned the old site and reported the new one ranking better on few backlinks, but the honest read is that it was simply fresh and unclassified (seranking.com) [single-source, weak]. **The leak's decision rule:** `hostAge` sandboxing, `createdDate`/`expiredDate` tracking registration history, and `homepagePagerankNs` seeding new pages from homepage authority together mean a new domain starts with a deliberate delay and no inherited trust (ipullrank.com), so a fresh domain trades a possibly-reversible classifier problem for a guaranteed cold start. Prefer rehab unless the domain carries a manual action you cannot remediate, the brand itself is the liability, or the entire URL inventory is programmatic content you would delete anyway. **The middle path that usually wins:** keep the domain, delete or noindex the offending inventory rather than rewriting it, keep and improve pages that still earn clicks, redirect removed URLs to their nearest surviving relative. This is the shape of most documented partial recoveries, though nobody has published a controlled comparison [contested].

## What is contested

- **Whether recovery from a site-wide quality classifier is realistically achievable.** The 21%-at-11-months figure is the strongest public data and comes from one practitioner's panel using third-party traffic estimates, not GSC. Haynes in March 2024 had seen none; Gabe in June 2025 documented real surges. Same population, different times. Honest statement: possible, rare, slow, usually partial.
- **Whether content freshness helps or hurts.** Zyppy's 0.455 correlation for winners having older last-updated dates cuts against standard refresh advice, but is small-sample, correlational, and confounded by the fact that sites which compulsively re-date usually do other things wrong.
- **Whether title CTR work does anything outside the click zone.** Quaid says no; SearchPilot's title tests win repeatedly, but on high-traffic templated pages already ranking. Both can be true: title work is a click-zone intervention, not a ranking intervention.
- **Whether AI Overviews raise or lower CTR at position 1.** Flying Cat reports 46.88% with AIOs versus 35.17% without, inverting the prevailing suppression narrative. Single-source and contradicted by many studies. Do not forecast on it without reproducing it on your own GSC data.
- **Whether public volatility indexes are worth reading at all.** The June 2026 divergence is a real structural critique, but running your own index needs a tracked keyword set large enough to be stable, which most SaaS sites lack. Defensible position: public indexes answer "is it everyone" and nothing else.
- **Whether the leak changed anything operationally.** King says it should have and did not; the counter-position, that it mostly confirmed existing practice, is also widely held. Both camps agree it describes 2024 internals with no guarantee about 2026 systems, which is the real limit on how hard anyone should lean on it.
- **Whether split-test results transfer between sites.** SearchPilot's own data argues no, given the same change producing +13% in one market and nothing in another. Quoting "brand-first titles give you 15%" as best practice misuses the source.

## Agent playbook notes

Assume an agent with SERP, keyword, backlink, on-page and GSC-style data tools, Postgres, and cron.

**Daily.** Pull GSC rows (API with pagination, or BigQuery export tables) into Postgres partitioned by date and keyed on query + page + country + device; never store only the aggregate, and track anonymized-query share as its own series so you know how blind you are. Recompute a private volatility index over your own tracked keywords, per country, per device, per page-type cluster, storing the distribution (median, p90, share of keywords moving more than N positions) rather than the mean, because the mean is what makes public indexes blind. Run the three-way demand/CTR/ranking decomposition on every material drop and alert only on the ranking branch, above a page-type threshold. Tag every data point with the update window it falls inside so later analyses never have to reconstruct it.

**Weekly.** Cannibalization sweep: for each query above an impression floor, list every URL taking impressions, flag queries where two or more alternate, then re-run over 7 days only to strip Google's rotation tests. Pure SQL once the rows are in Postgres. Click-zone classification: bucket every query+page pair, surface CTR recommendations only inside the zone and ranking recommendations outside. Regex segment refresh, auto-proposing new brand misspellings from the non-brand residue.

**Monthly.** Re-derive the site's own CTR-by-position curve from GSC rows, segmented by intent and by AI Overview presence, and overwrite the benchmark curve in the forecast model; this single job removes the largest source of forecast error. Re-run the three-scenario forecast and flag ±15% variance. Maintain a fixed cohort panel in the Gabe pattern so the next update has a baseline instead of a scramble.

**Per update, event-driven.** On a confirmed rollout start, freeze a 28-day pre-window snapshot and do not analyze during the rollout; collect signal inside the wait window and defer decisions until at least a week after completion. Enforce "movement before the announced date is not this update" in code, since it is the most common attribution error. Run the delta report: fetch current SERPs for top lost queries and classify the page types now ranking, because relevancy shift versus quality demotion forks everything downstream and needs SERP data, not just GSC. Check the Manual Actions report first, every time.

**Propose but do not execute.** Split tests: an agent can bucket, forecast and test significance, but deployment is human, and below roughly 30,000 monthly sessions on a page group it should refuse to call significance and report directionally. Also off-limits: reconsideration requests, content deletion at scale, domain migration, any change to URL inventory, and the forecast number that goes in a board deck.

**Never.** Quote macro CTR or macro average position. Treat GSC API output as a complete query set without labelling the anonymization rate. Attribute a change to a named update without checking the announced start against the observed inflection. Recommend a tactic on the basis of somebody else's split test.
